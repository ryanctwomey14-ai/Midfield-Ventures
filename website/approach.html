Our investment strategy.

We buy one kind of property, in one kind of market, with one kind of business plan. Everything below is what we look for — and, just as usefully, what makes us walk away.

Learn more → How investing works

We play the middle of the field.

New construction almost exclusively targets the top of the market, because that is the only place construction costs pencil. The consequence is that the supply of middle-market housing is effectively fixed while demand for it keeps growing — and every time rents at the top get out of reach, more households move down into it.

That gives Class B and C apartments a structural advantage that shows up precisely when you want it: in a soft year, demand moves toward us rather than away.

We would rather own an ordinary building at a sensible basis than an extraordinary one at a price that requires everything to go right.

What we target.

Every criterion below exists to take a specific risk off the table before your capital is in the deal. Size keeps us out of bidding wars with institutional buyers. Occupancy means we are buying a mismanaged building rather than an empty one. Fixed-rate debt means a rate move cannot force a sale at the wrong moment.

A property has to clear all of it, not most of it. When a deal fails one line we do not widen the criteria to accommodate it — that is how sponsors end up owning the thing they said they would never buy, with someone else’s money in it.

We review these annually, and whenever market conditions change materially.

What we will not buy.

Most properties we underwrite get a no. These are the reasons, and they are not negotiable when a deal is otherwise attractive — which is exactly when they matter.

A deal that only works on projected rents

If the debt service is not comfortable at today’s income, the business plan is a hope. We underwrite what the property earns now, then treat the improvement as upside rather than as the plan.

Floating-rate debt without a real hedge

The 2021–22 vintage taught this lesson at other people’s expense. Where bridge debt is genuinely the right tool, it comes with a rate cap that runs the full term and reserves sized for the cap to be needed.

Structural or environmental problems we cannot price

Tim walks every property before we bid. Foundation movement, failing plumbing stacks, aluminium wiring, or anything requiring remediation we cannot scope confidently is a pass — not a discount negotiation.

A submarket with a supply wave coming

We count the units under construction and permitted within a defined radius. If the pipeline can absorb our renovation premium, the renovation premium is not real.

A market where the rules can change mid-plan

Rent control exposure, eviction regimes that make collections unworkable, or a regulatory direction that punishes improving a property. The returns are not worth the tail risk.

A deal we would not put our own money into

We invest in every property we sponsor. That is not only an alignment statement — it is a filter, and it removes deals that survive every spreadsheet test but still feel wrong.

What happens after we buy.

The renovation is the part of the business plan most likely to go wrong, which is why it is the part we control directly rather than delegate and hope.

Work is funded from reserves raised at closing, not from operating cash flow, so improving the property never competes with paying partners.

Once the property is stabilised, the cash it produces after expenses and the mortgage is paid out to investors quarterly. That is the passive income — it arrives whether or not you do anything, and it comes from rent collected on real leases rather than from selling anything.

Months 0–3

Take control

New property management installed, staffing reviewed, delinquency addressed, expenses re-bid. A meaningful share of the value in a mismanaged property is recovered here before a single unit is renovated.

Months 1–6

Exteriors and common areas

Curb appeal, lighting, landscaping, amenity spaces and signage. This is what changes who tours the property, and it has to happen before interior premiums can be achieved.

Months 3–24

Interiors, on natural turnover

Units are renovated as residents move out rather than by displacing them — slower, but it avoids the vacancy loss and the reputational damage that come with emptying a building.

Months 6–60

Operate and distribute

Stabilised operations, monthly financials to the partnership, and quarterly distributions.

Years 3–7

Refinance or sell

We exit when the numbers say to, not on a fixed calendar. Holding longer beats selling into a bad market, and the operating agreement allows for it.

What makes a market qualify.

We source underperforming assets in emerging markets, where growth is on the horizon but has not yet been priced in.
