Four engines, running at the same time.

Apartment returns do not come from one thing. They come from four, and understanding them is the difference between investing and speculating.

01

Cash flow

Rent, minus operating costs and the mortgage, is distributed to owners. This is the part that shows up in your account while you are doing something else. It is also the part that makes a bad year survivable rather than fatal.

02

Forced appreciation

This is the one people miss. A house is worth what similar houses sold for. An apartment building is worth its net operating income divided by a market cap rate — so raising income or cutting expenses raises the value, on purpose, without waiting for the market to cooperate.

03

Debt paydown

Residents' rent pays down the loan every month. Equity accumulates quietly whether or not values move, and by the time the property is sold a meaningful share of the original balance has been retired by someone else's money.

04

Tax treatment

Depreciation lets you report a loss on an asset that is not actually losing value. Cost segregation accelerates it. The result is that reported taxable income is often far lower than the cash you received — sometimes negative.

Why $100 of rent is worth more than $100.

Commercial property is valued on income. If buildings in a market trade at a 6% cap rate, then every extra dollar of annual net operating income adds roughly $16.67 of value — one divided by the cap rate.

That is why a renovation programme matters so much. Raising rents by $150 a month across 100 units is $180,000 of additional annual income. At a 6% cap rate, that is $3 million of created value — on a capital project that might have cost a fraction of it.

It also cuts the other way, which is the part sponsors skip. If cap rates rise because interest rates rise, the same income is worth less. That is exactly what happened to a great many 2021 acquisitions, and it is why the entry price and the debt structure matter more than the renovation plan.

Apartments are graded roughly by age, finish and location. The grade determines who lives there, and who lives there determines how the asset behaves in a downturn.

What can go wrong, stated plainly.

If a sponsor's education page has no risk section, that is the most important thing on it.

Interest rates and cap rates

Rising rates raise borrowing costs and push cap rates up, which lowers value on identical income. Deals bought at peak pricing with floating-rate debt in 2021–22 are the cautionary tale of this cycle.

Leverage

Debt amplifies both directions. A 25% fall in value can erase far more than 25% of the equity, and the lender is paid before you are.

Execution

Renovation budgets overrun. Timelines slip. A property manager underperforms. The business plan is a plan, not a result.

Local supply

A market can absorb thousands of new units and turn a good submarket soft for two years. Concessions follow, and projected rents do not materialise.

Illiquidity

There is no exit button. Your capital is committed for the full hold, which may be extended if selling into a bad market would harm partners.

Sponsor risk

You are trusting people. An inexperienced or dishonest sponsor can lose the asset regardless of how good the property was. Diligence the operator at least as hard as the building.

Private real estate can lose some or all of your investment. Nothing on this page is a projection, a recommendation, or an offer to sell a security.

The words you will meet in an offering.

If someone uses one of these to make you feel behind, find a different sponsor.

Net operating income (NOI)
All property income minus all operating expenses, before the mortgage. The number a building's value is calculated from.
Cap rate
NOI divided by price. A 6% cap rate means the property produces 6% of its price in annual income before debt. Lower cap rate, higher price.
Cash-on-cash return
Annual cash distributed divided by cash invested. What actually lands in your account each year, as a percentage.
Equity multiple
Total dollars returned divided by dollars invested. A 1.8x multiple on $100,000 returns $180,000 in total across the hold.
Internal rate of return (IRR)
An annualised return that accounts for when you received money. Money returned sooner produces a higher IRR than the same money returned later.
Preferred return
The annual return limited partners receive before the sponsor participates in profits. Usually accrues if unpaid.
Promote / carried interest
The sponsor's share of profits above the preferred return. The mechanism that aligns the sponsor with you.
Waterfall
The order in which money is distributed: lender, then preferred return, then return of capital, then the profit split.
Value-add
Buying a physically or operationally underperforming property and improving it, rather than buying a finished one and holding.
Cost segregation
An engineering study that reclassifies parts of a building into shorter depreciation lives, accelerating deductions into the early years.
Capital call
A request for additional money from partners when a property needs more than it has. Rare, unwelcome, and disclosed as a possibility in every offering.
Accredited investor
An SEC definition based on income, net worth or professional licence. Most private offerings are limited to accredited investors.
Bridge debt
Short-term, usually floating-rate financing used during a renovation. Flexible, and the source of most distress in this cycle.
Agency debt
Longer-term, usually fixed-rate loans backed by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. Less flexible, far more durable.
